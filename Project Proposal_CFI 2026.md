**The Price of Interoperability: Sustainable Financing Models for Zambia’s Digital Payment Infrastructure** 

## **PI: Dr Michael Chanda Chiseni** 

## **Co-PIs: Dr. Patricia Funjikwa and Mr. Philip Lingililani Chulu** 

## **1. Project Summary** 

The National Financial Switch (NFS) of Zambia operates as a national payment network which is operated by the Zambia Electronic Clearing House Limited (ZECHL) and jointly governed by the Bank of Zambia (BOZ) together with some participating commercial banks (Bank of Zambia, 2023). As with many Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) components in low- and middle-income countries, the NFS was established with significant state and external funding support (AfricaNenda, World Bank, & UNECA, 2024). Yet its long-term viability depends on whether costs, authority, and incentives can be distributed sustainably across the central bank, ZECHL, commercial banks, mobile network operators, and fintechs without locking the system into permanent subsidy or compromising the affordability gains it was designed to deliver. 

In a context where mobile money transactions reached an estimated 452 billion kwacha in 2023 (UNDP, 2024), this project investigates which funding and institutional models can sustain Zambia's NFS over time by analysing its governance structure, cost-allocation arrangements, pricing rules, and cost-recovery mechanisms. Crucially, this research will explore the inherent tension between implementing sustainable revenue models for ongoing maintenance whilst preserving the low-cost access necessary to protect financial inclusion gains. 

## **2. Background and Problem Statement** 

In most low- and middle-income countries, DPI is often initiated via hybrid public-private funding or multilateral grants. And, while donor funding enables the initial construction phase, it rarely covers long-term operational expenses, including server maintenance, cybersecurity upgrades, API enhancements, and skilled labour costs (Eaves & Kedia, 2024). In the absence of sustainable funding structures, DPI systems are vulnerable to technological decay and declining operational reliability (Totolo, 2025). Yet the alternative steep switching fees levied on Payment Service Providers (PSPs) and end-users to recover maintenance costs, risks reversing progress on financial inclusion. Global evidence suggests that when instant payment schemes mandate free or near-free transactions for end-users, the burden of operational costs 

is disproportionately absorbed by PSPs, an arrangement that becomes financially unsustainable without government subsidies or viable cross-selling revenue models (Porteous, 2025). 

These challenges are institutional as much as they are financial. The worldwide expansion of retail instant payment systems has created new pressure on both regulators and operators to resolve how payment infrastructure is funded and how costs are shared across participants (Doerr, Gambacorta, & Tissot, 2024). This sustainability problem is prevalent across Africa, where most national switches experience ongoing financing gaps and uneven levels of private sector involvement (AfricaNenda, World Bank, & UNECA, 2024). Ostrom (1990) argues that common-pool resources, those shared by multiple actors who each benefit, but none fully owns, require clear cost-sharing rules, agreed contribution obligations, and collective monitoring mechanisms to avoid free-riding and long-term underfunding. Zambia’s NFS functions as a shared resource. Its hybrid ownership structure, in which the BOZ and commercial banks jointly own ZECHL, creates inherent tensions over pricing authority, contribution obligations, and the distribution of infrastructure costs among participants, tensions that remain poorly defined and understudied. 

Zambia therefore presents an ideal context to study this problem. The country operates a highly active digital finance sector underpinned by a state-backed interoperability switch the NFS operated by ZECHL (AfricaNenda, World Bank, & UNECA, 2024; Bank of Zambia, 2023). However, there is a lack of empirical evidence on how the financial burden of operating this infrastructure is allocated across key participants, including the Bank of Zambia, ZECHL, commercial banks, mobile network operators, and fintechs, and whether the current arrangements are economically sustainable in the long-term. 

To fill this gap, the project addresses the following central research question ‘What institutional model is best suited to achieve financial sustainability for Zambia’s NFS, given its hybrid ownership structure, regulatory environment, and the affordability goals that underpin its public value mandate? 

This question is addressed through three sub-questions: 

1. What are the operational costs of maintaining Zambia’s NFS, and how are these currently allocated across the Bank of Zambia, ZECHL, commercial banks, mobile network operators, and fintechs? 

2. How do existing pricing guidelines, authority structures, and end-user payment expectations affect participants’ incentives to contribute to long-term cost recovery? 

3. What institutional models exist to achieve financial sustainability for Zambia’s NFS while preserving the affordability goals that underpin its public value mandate? 

The project directly addresses the CFI’s 2026 Call by generating locally grounded, empirical evidence on how Zambia can transition its NFS from donor-subsidized capital expenditure to a financially viable, self-sustaining operational model. Therefore, by grounding the analysis in Zambia’s specific institutional and market realities, the research will help policymakers design governance arrangements that better balance public value, commercial sustainability, and system integrity. 

## **3. Methodology and Expected Output** 

This research will employ a mixed-methods case study approach implemented over eight months. The project will combine financial document analysis with qualitative stakeholder interviews and economic scenario modelling. 

**Phase 1: Document and Financial Data Analysis:** Review of the BOZ’s NPS Annual Reports, AfricaNenda's SIIPS 2024, national budget allocations for ZECHL, and available multilateral grant frameworks to construct a baseline financial model of the NFS’s operational costs and current revenue streams. 

**Phase 2: Semi-Structured Key Informant Interviews:** Approximately 25–30 interviews across three stakeholder tiers: 

_Regulators and operators:_ BOZ and ZECHL leadership, to understand long-term institutional strategies and public funding constraints. 

_PSPs:_ Executives at commercial banks, mobile network operators, and fintechs, to understand how connectivity and compliance costs are absorbed or passed on to consumers. 

_Development partners:_ Representatives from multilateral organisations supporting Zambia’s digital transformation, to assess planned exit strategies for infrastructure grants. 

**Phase 3: Economic Modelling and Scenario Building:** Using data from Phases 1 and 2, three financing scenarios will be modelled, a Public Utility Model, a Cost-Recovery Consortium, and a Freemium Cross-Subsidisation Model, each evaluated against financial viability and user affordability.

2. A Policy Brief 

3. A Dissemination Workshop: Hosted in Lusaka with relevant stakeholders.
