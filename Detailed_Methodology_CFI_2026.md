Detailed Methodology
1. Research Design and Rationale
This study adopts a mixed-methods, single-country case study design implemented over eight months, with Zambia's National Financial Switch (NFS) as the unit of analysis. A case study approach is appropriate because the research question is explanatory rather than descriptive: it asks not only how the costs of operating the NFS are currently distributed, but why the prevailing arrangements generate the incentives they do, and what alternative institutional models could reconcile financial sustainability with the affordability goals that underpin the system's public value mandate. These are questions about mechanisms, motivations, and institutional design that cannot be answered through quantitative cost data alone, nor through stakeholder perceptions in isolation.

The design therefore deliberately integrates three complementary strands of evidence. Quantitative financial data establish the magnitude and structure of the NFS's operating costs and revenue streams; qualitative key informant interviews surface the institutional logics, incentives, and constraints that shape how those costs are negotiated and absorbed; and economic scenario modelling translates this combined evidence into forward-looking, testable financing options. Each strand addresses a distinct weakness of the others, and their convergence (or divergence) is itself treated as a finding. This triangulated approach responds directly to the gap identified in the background: that there is presently little empirical evidence on how the financial burden of operating Zambia's interoperability infrastructure is allocated across the Bank of Zambia (BOZ), ZECHL, commercial banks, mobile network operators, and fintechs, and whether those arrangements are sustainable over the long term.

The analysis is organised conceptually around Ostrom's (1990) treatment of common-pool resources. Because the NFS is jointly owned and used by multiple actors who each benefit from interoperability but none of whom fully owns the underlying infrastructure, it is vulnerable to the classic problems of free-riding and chronic underfunding unless cost-sharing rules, contribution obligations, and collective monitoring mechanisms are clearly defined. This framework structures both the data we collect and the criteria against which we evaluate alternative financing models, ensuring that the empirical work speaks consistently to the central research question and its three sub-questions.

2. Phase 1: Document and Financial Data Analysis (Month 1)
The first phase establishes a baseline financial model of the NFS. We will systematically review the Bank of Zambia's National Payment Systems (NPS) Annual Reports, AfricaNenda's State of Inclusive Instant Payment Systems in Africa (SIIPS 2024), publicly available ZECHL budget and governance documentation, national budget allocations relevant to payment systems, and the grant and concessional-financing frameworks through which the switch and related DPI components were originally capitalised. Where published figures are incomplete, the gaps will be documented and subsequently addressed through the key informant interviews in Phase 2, so that the two phases are mutually reinforcing rather than sequential in a strict sense.

From these sources we will construct a structured cost inventory that distinguishes the NFS's capital expenditure (the original build and periodic upgrades) from its recurrent operational expenditure, the latter disaggregated into the cost categories that the literature identifies as the principal drivers of long-run sustainability: server and infrastructure maintenance, cybersecurity and resilience upgrades, API development and enhancement, settlement and liquidity arrangements, and skilled labour (Eaves & Kedia, 2024). Against this cost base we will map the corresponding revenue and contribution streams, including switching and interchange fees, participant contributions, and any direct or implicit public subsidy. The output of this phase is an inception report containing the conceptual framework and a baseline financial model that makes explicit, for the first time, how the cost of operating the NFS is currently shared among participants. This baseline becomes the empirical foundation for the scenario modelling in Phase 3. Ethical clearance from the University of Zambia will be applied for during this period.

3. Phase 2: Semi-Structured Key Informant Interviews (Month 2)
The second phase generates primary qualitative data through approximately 25–30 semi-structured key informant interviews. Respondents will be selected through purposive sampling, supplemented by snowball referrals, to ensure coverage of the actors who hold decision-making authority over, or bear the costs of, the NFS. Sampling will continue until thematic saturation is reached within each tier. Mr Chulu's position within the Bank of Zambia's Payment Systems Oversight unit provides direct access to the relevant institutional networks, which substantially de-risks recruitment of senior respondents.

Interviews will be organised across three stakeholder tiers, mirroring the structure of the cost-allocation problem:

Regulators and operators. Leadership and technical staff at the BOZ and ZECHL, to understand long-term institutional strategy, pricing authority, public funding constraints, and the governance arrangements that determine how contribution obligations are set and monitored.

Payment Service Providers (PSPs). Executives and product or finance leads at commercial banks, mobile network operators, and fintechs, to establish how the costs of connectivity, compliance, and participation are absorbed internally or passed on to consumers, and how current pricing guidelines and end-user expectations shape their willingness to contribute to long-term cost recovery.

Development partners. Representatives of the multilateral and bilateral organisations supporting Zambia's digital transformation, to assess the planned exit strategies for infrastructure grants and the assumptions those partners hold about the transition to operational self-sufficiency.

A tailored interview guide will be developed for each tier from a common analytical spine derived from the three sub-questions, so that responses remain comparable across respondents while allowing tier-specific probing. Indicative themes include the perceived drivers of operating cost, the fairness and clarity of existing cost-sharing rules, the points of tension created by the NFS's hybrid ownership structure, and the conditions under which each participant would accept a revised contribution model. Interviews will be conducted in person where feasible and remotely otherwise, audio-recorded with consent, and transcribed for analysis.

4. Phase 3: Economic Modelling and Scenario Building (Months 3–4)
The third phase synthesises the financial baseline from Phase 1 and the institutional evidence from Phase 2 into a comparative assessment of three financing scenarios for the NFS:

A Public Utility Model, in which the switch is treated as essential public infrastructure with operating costs met primarily through central provision, regulated tariffs, or a dedicated levy, and pricing held low in service of financial inclusion.

A Cost-Recovery Consortium, in which participants jointly fund operations through transparent, rules-based contributions proportionate to usage or capacity, consistent with the cost-sharing and collective-monitoring principles that Ostrom (1990) identifies as conditions for the sustainable governance of shared resources.

A Freemium Cross-Subsidisation Model, in which a tier of low-value or end-user transactions is kept free or near-free to protect inclusion, while higher-value, premium, or value-added services generate the surplus required to sustain the system.

Each scenario will be specified as a transparent financial model, parameterised using the cost categories and revenue assumptions assembled in Phase 1 and the behavioural and institutional constraints elicited in Phase 2. The models will project operating costs and revenues over a multi-year horizon and will be assessed against two explicit and competing criteria: financial viability (whether the model closes the operating funding gap without reliance on continued donor subsidy) and user affordability (whether it preserves the low-cost access on which financial inclusion gains depend). Because several key parameters are uncertain, particularly transaction-volume growth and participants' price sensitivity, we will conduct sensitivity analysis around the central assumptions, allowing us to identify which model is most robust under a range of plausible conditions rather than under a single point estimate. This component will be led by Dr Funjika, drawing on her macroeconomic and financial-modelling expertise.

5. Data Analysis and Integration
Qualitative data will be analysed thematically using NVivo, combining a deductive coding frame derived from the three sub-questions and the common-pool resource framework with inductive codes that emerge from the transcripts. Coding will be documented to maintain an auditable analytical trail, and emerging themes will be cross-checked among the research team to strengthen interpretive reliability. The quantitative and qualitative strands will then be integrated through triangulation: the financial baseline disciplines the interview evidence with hard cost figures, while the interview evidence explains the institutional reasons behind the patterns observed in the financial data and supplies the behavioural assumptions that parameterise the scenario models. Points at which the two strands disagree, for instance where stated cost-sharing rules diverge from observed contribution patterns, will be treated as analytically significant and reported as such.

6. Ethical Considerations and Data Management
The project will obtain ethical clearance from the University of Zambia prior to the commencement of fieldwork. Participation will be voluntary and based on informed consent, with respondents free to withdraw at any stage. Given that several respondents will be senior figures discussing commercially and institutionally sensitive matters, particular care will be taken to protect confidentiality: interview data will be anonymised, findings will be reported at a level of aggregation that prevents the identification of individuals or firms, and any commercially sensitive financial information shared in confidence will be handled accordingly. Data will be stored securely with restricted access for the duration of the project and managed in line with the requirements of the grantee institution, the Southern African Institute for Policy and Research (SAIPAR).

7. Methodological Limitations
Two limitations are acknowledged at the outset. First, some elements of the NFS's cost and revenue structure may be commercially confidential and only partially disclosed; we mitigate this through triangulation across documentary sources and multiple independent respondents, and by reporting ranges where precise figures cannot be verified. Second, as a single-country case study, the findings are most directly applicable to Zambia; however, because the underlying sustainability problem and the common-pool dynamics it reflects are common to national switches across Africa (AfricaNenda, World Bank, & UNECA, 2024), the analytical framework and the comparative evaluation of financing models are designed to be transferable to comparable contexts.

8. Expected Outputs
The methodology yields three deliverables that map directly onto the research question and sub-questions: a research report (subsequently developed into a publication) presenting the baseline cost-allocation analysis and the comparative assessment of financing models; a policy brief distilling the implications for the BOZ, ZECHL, and participating PSPs; and a dissemination workshop in Lusaka convening regulators, operators, PSPs, and development partners to discuss the findings and the viable pathways towards a financially sustainable NFS.
